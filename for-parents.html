The other five days

Class worked. Now what happens between classes?

You signed them up and it worked. Two nights a week they come out of the dojang lit up, talking the whole way home and showing you a kick in the kitchen before their shoes are off.

Then there are the other five days.

You can see what another ten minutes would do. Not because you’re pushing. They want to be good at this and so do you. But a sixty-minute class split fifteen ways leaves only so much time for individual correction, and the reps that make a roundhouse automatic have to come from somewhere else.

“Go practice” is where it falls apart

They do four kicks in the living room, lose interest, and ask for the iPad. Or they ask what to work on, and you hit one of two walls.

Either you don’t know, because you’ve never thrown a roundhouse in your life and you’re not about to start correcting one. Or you do know. You tell them. And they don’t want to hear it from you.

I train, so the second one is mine. A grade-school kid will argue with a parent about a kick and take the same two words off a card without blinking. That isn’t a flaw in your kid. It’s what being eight is.

Your kid was always willing. “Go practice” asks them to be their own coach, and it puts you in a job no parent wins.

The idea

Nobody needs to be the coach.
The card is.

Every card holds one drill: the name, the reps, and what a good rep looks like, in words a kid can read on their own.

Stay the parent

The knowledge is already on the card.

Drill 1: Shuffle Roundtrips. Ten roundtrips. Hands up. Bounce and shuffle. Silent feet.

That’s a coach’s instruction, written down. Nobody has to remember what the instructor said on Tuesday, and nobody has to invent a session out of nothing.

If you have never trained, the card knows and you don’t have to. If you have trained, the card says it so you don’t have to be the one saying it. Either way, you get to stay the parent.

Two ways to use the deck

Do it with them, or hand it over.

Both work. Most families end up doing some of each.

01

Run it together

They pull the cards, you read the drill out and count, and you hold the paddle when a card needs one. You’re participating, not teaching.

02

Let them take the box

Most kids get there eventually. A kid training on a Sunday afternoon because they felt like it, with nobody watching and nobody asking, is the outcome you want.

Choice changes the session

Let them pick, and the arguing stops.

Tell a kid to go practice and you’ve handed them an order. Hand them the deck and ask them to pull five cards and you’ve handed them choice. Same ten minutes. Completely different afternoon.

The choice is real: 65 drills, nine categories, no wrong pick. Shuffle, deal five, go. Some days they’ll pull the five easiest cards in the deck. Fine. They still trained.

When it’s fun, they do more than you asked.

You set out five cards and they do eight. They want the paddle one again. They go back to a drill they already finished because they know it was sloppy and they want the Stretch Star clean.

That’s the engine. Not discipline applied from outside. A kid who wants another go.

The card sets the problem. They work out the answer.

A card says “silent feet.” It doesn’t say how. So they try it. Too heavy. Lighter, and they lose their balance. Again, with their weight farther forward, and it works. Now it belongs to them in a way it never would have if you had stood there explaining it.

Give a kid 65 named drills and sooner or later they start combining them. By then, they aren’t doing a session you set. They’re training themselves, which is the only kind that survives you leaving the room.

How you actually use them

Three steps. About ten minutes.

A kid can run all three without you.

  1. 1

    Pick five cards

    Choose one category or a spread across the deck. A choice they made beats a “perfect” session somebody made for them.

  2. 2

    Do the drill

    The reps are on the front. Leave the card where they can see it, so nobody has to carry the instructions in their head.

  3. 3

    Earn the stars

    Two goals are for doing the drill right. The Stretch Star is for doing it clean. Mark the tracker when the card is done.

Questions parents ask

Before you hand over the deck.

Should I do it with them, or let them do it alone?

Either. Younger kids usually want you in the room reading the card and counting. Older kids often take the box and go. Most families do both.

I’ve never done taekwondo. Can I still run this?

Yes. Every card names the drill, the reps, and what a good rep looks like. You hand over the box and the card does the rest.

I train. Shouldn’t I just coach them myself?

You can. The cards still help because the problem was never your knowledge. Let the card give the instruction so you can keep the evening.

My kid is six. Is that too young?

The deck is built for roughly ages 6 to 13. The reps scale: a younger student can do fewer, while an older student chases the Stretch Star.

Will this contradict what their instructor teaches?

These are conditioning and repetition drills: footwork, kicks, combinations, reaction, and related skills. There are no forms or style-specific techniques.

What equipment do we need?

Most cards need none. A few call for a paddle, kick shield, or BlazePods, and they are marked so you can skip or swap them.

What if they get bored?

They can pick different cards or stop for a week. Nothing expires and nothing resets. That is part of the point.

How long is a session?

About ten minutes for five cards. Some kids do three. Some do nine.
