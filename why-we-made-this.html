Why we made this

We made these for our daughter on her grandparents’ kitchen table, over Christmas.

The dojang closed for the holidays. We had a driveway, a living room, one paddle, a sofa cushion, and a kid who still wanted to train.

The problem

“Go practice” didn’t work for us either.

Sofia had been training four times a week, including two sparring sessions with the competition team. Three days into the holiday break, she did four kicks and stopped. Then she asked what she should work on.

I train, so I told her. She didn’t want to hear it from me.

Experiment02

The accidental solution

So we wrote the drills on index cards.

One drill per card, the reps on the front, and something to aim for. She picked five a day and did them in her grandparents’ living room or driveway.

She took every word of it because it came off a card and not out of my mouth. That is the whole idea, and we found it by accident.

What changed

The training became specific—and hers.

She came back from that break better than she left. Nothing clever about it. The training didn’t need to be long. It needed to be specific, and it needed to belong to her.

☆ Clear work☆ Daily reps★ Self-directed

Version two

The first deck was paper, and it did not survive.

Cards get dropped. They get stepped on. Somebody puts a water bottle down on them. Within a few weeks ours were falling apart.

So we laminated them. Then we laminated the next version onto heavier stock. The finished deck uses 14pt card stock, gloss laminated on both sides, with rounded corners so the cards do not fray in a gear bag.

The box exists for the same unglamorous reason. It has to close, survive being thrown in with pads and a jump rope, and still be findable on a shelf six months later.

None of that is exciting. It’s just what happens to a deck of cards somebody actually uses.

Then people started asking

A homemade deck stopped looking like a joke.

Parents at our studio wanted to know what Sofia was doing. A coach asked. The honest answer was a deck of index cards and ten minutes a day—until we showed them.

That is not a promise about anybody else’s child. Her coaches, sparring partners, and the hours she put in at the dojang all went into that season. The cards were the part that worked when the dojang was shut.

What she actually got out of it

Three things that are hard to practice in a crowded class.

01

Discipline

Discipline is doing it when nobody is making you. A deck on the shelf is an invitation a kid can take or leave. The Sunday she picked it up with no class that week was hers, and she knew it.

★ Chose to begin
02

Creativity

Creativity arrived once she knew the pieces. With 65 named drills, she started combining them: front leg, follow, back kick. That is the thinking behind sparring.

★ Built something new
03

Confidence

Confidence is not being told you are good. It is walking onto the mat having done the reps and knowing it. The tracker is the record, in her handwriting, not ours.

★ Knew the work was done
